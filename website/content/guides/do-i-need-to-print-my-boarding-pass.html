When a phone is enough

For most flights, once you've checked in online the airline offers a mobile boarding pass: a QR or barcode in its app, in an email, or as a pass in Apple Wallet or Google Wallet. Security staff and gate agents scan it straight from your screen.

If the airline offers you a mobile pass at check-in, it means it's accepted for that flight. If it only offers a PDF "to print", read the instructions: some PDFs can be shown on a phone, some must be printed.

When you may still need paper

  • Some routes and airports don't accept mobile passes, and the airline will tell you at check-in.
  • Document checks. On some international routes, airlines must see your passport or visa at a desk before you board. You'll get a stamped or printed pass there. Low-cost airlines are especially likely to ask for this for certain passports.
  • Separate tickets. If your connection is on a different booking, or a different airline without a shared ticket, you need a boarding pass for each leg, and the second airline may only issue it at its own desk.
  • Infants on your lap sometimes get a separate pass that's issued at the desk.
Watch for airport check-in fees

Some low-cost airlines charge a fee to check in or print a boarding pass at the airport. If your airline is one of them, check in online and keep the pass on your phone, or print it at home.

How to add a boarding pass to your phone's wallet

  1. Check in online

    In the airline's app or website, as soon as check-in opens.

  2. Look for "Add to Apple Wallet" or "Save to Google Wallet"

    It's usually on the boarding pass screen, or as a button in the confirmation email.

  3. Open it once with signal

    Make sure the pass shows the barcode, then put your phone in aeroplane mode and open it again to check it works offline.

  4. Add one per traveller

    Each person, including children, has their own pass and barcode. Most wallet apps group passes for the same flight together.

Make sure it opens with no signal

The moment you need a boarding pass is usually the moment signal disappears: deep inside a terminal, at a gate, or in a queue with a thousand other phones. A pass that only exists as a link in an email won't help then. Before you leave home:

  • Save every pass to your wallet, or download the PDF to the phone itself.
  • Take a screenshot as a backup. It doesn't replace the pass, but staff can often read the barcode from it.
  • Charge your phone and carry a power bank. A dead phone is a lost boarding pass.
  • For a family, consider having two adults each hold everyone's passes.

Paper as a backup

If the airline allows it and you have a printer, a paper copy is a cheap backup for long journeys, connections and travelling with children. Keep it with the passports. It's also the easiest way to give a pass to a child who's old enough to hold their own.
